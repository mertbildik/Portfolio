# Writing — the site-wide voice

Research into plain-English craft, company copy, admired personal-site writing, and AI-writing
tells. This guides every string on the site: headlines, case studies, nav, CTAs, captions,
form microcopy, metadata. Researched Aug 2026; sources ranged from Ogilvy's 1982 memo and
Zinsser to current analyses of LLM prose.

## The register, located

The target register exists and has names attached:

- **Basecamp's homepage** is the closest company model: a speaking voice that addresses one
  reader, makes a claim, and immediately backs it with a number ("On average, each member of
  our customer service team has been with us 9 years"). Their doctrine: "Copywriting is
  interface design… every letter matters."
- **Frank Chimero's about page** is the closest personal model: "I helped design a few things
  you've probably used, like NPR's online audio player and Wikipedia's article pages."
  Credentials converted into the reader's own memory. "Helped design" is honest scoping.
  It ends "Thanks for looking."
- **Derek Sivers** shows the rhythm tool: flat declarative sentences, then a fragment doing
  emotional work ("That was the saddest thought to me: to stop moving. Still is."). And
  self-claims immediately complicated: "I'm not bragging about this. It kinda sucks."
- **Craig Mod** shows the rare word earning its seat: "collimate thought" — a precise optics
  term, used once, surrounded by ordinary words.
- Counter-model: **Stripe's 2026 homepage** ("implement custom revenue models") — precise but
  enterprise; admire their docs discipline, not their marketing headers. **Linear**'s
  discipline lives in verb-first section headings and plain buttons, while their taglines
  ("A new species of product tool") are exactly the positioning register to avoid.

## The principles that carry the most weight

1. **Write the way you talk, then edit** (Ogilvy). The read-aloud pass is the bug detector:
   stumble means rewrite. Sleep on it; edit next morning.
2. **The reader's energy is a budget** (Graham). Simplicity is reader economics. And it's
   honesty enforcement: "If you say nothing simply, it will be obvious to everyone, including
   you." A claim that survives translation into plain speech is a real claim.
3. **Active voice, named actor.** On a portfolio, passive voice reads as credit-dodging.
   "The navigation was simplified" → "I cut the nav from nine items to four."
4. **Concrete beats abstract.** The proof lives inside the sentence — a name, a number, a
   thing that happened — or the sentence goes.
5. **Cut at the phrase level** (Zinsser): "in order to" → "to", "utilized" → "used",
   "the fact that" → cut, adverbs that repeat the verb → cut.
6. **No jargon** (Ogilvy: "hallmarks of a pretentious ass"). Portfolio equivalents:
   "ideation," "learnings," "stakeholder alignment," "north star," "leverage."
7. **Rules are taste training, not a lint pass.** Orwell's own rule 6: "Break any of these
   rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." Plainness without rhythm, specificity,
   and stance is just beige — "lots of people write simply and they're terrible."

## Precision vs. simplicity — the tension the brief cares about

- The verb carries the precision. "The toggle swaps languages" beats "enables multilingual
  functionality" because *swap* is what happens: two states, instant, reversible. Apple
  institutionalized this — "turn on," never "enable"; the phrasal verb a person would say
  beats the formal verb a system would log.
- **The mime test for verbs**: swap, cut, moved, rebuilt, shipped, drew — a reader could act
  them out. Enable, enhance, drive, streamline — no.
- **McPhee's discipline**: when a word isn't right, hunt in the dictionary, not the thesaurus —
  the thesaurus gives you a different word, the dictionary gives you the exact one.
- **When the rare word earns its place**: when it's the only word that means the thing, used
  once, in a plain sentence. "Kerning" to a founder is precision; "synergize" is costume.
  Never two rare words in one sentence.

## AI tells and the human alternative

The machine's signature is *uniformity* — same rhythm, same scaffold, same enthusiasm
everywhere. Any single device once is human; the tell is frequency.

| Tell | Human alternative |
|---|---|
| Rule-of-three everywhere ("innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking") | One exact adjective, or none. Real lists have the number of items reality has. |
| "It's not X, it's Y" scaffolds; "no X, no Y, just Z" | State Y. If the contrast matters, name the actual wrong belief. Once per site, fine; once per page, machine. |
| Prestige vocabulary (delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, seamless, robust, elevate, showcase, streamline, foster) | The plain verb or the concrete fact. Avoid the *behavior* (reaching for prestige words), not just today's blacklist — the list moves. |
| Em-dash density ("the ChatGPT hyphen") | Keep it for the one place it lands; periods and commas elsewhere. Matches the brief: no em dashes as a default. |
| Uniform sentence rhythm (metronomic 14–22 words) | Burst on purpose: a long sentence that earns a short one. "Still is." |
| Hedge + hype in one breath | Commit. Own uncertainty only where it's real and specific. |
| Summary closers ("Ultimately," "In summary," recap paragraphs) | End on the last new thing — the result, or the honest residue. |
| Copula avoidance ("serves as," "stands as," "represents") | Let "is" be the verb. "This is a booking tool for barbers" is confident. |
| Punchline-itis ("But here's the kicker") | Most sentences just carry information. One earned punchline per page. |
| Generic profundity ("Change is the only constant") | Only claims that could be false, about things actually done. If the opposite is absurd ("I care about craft"), delete it. |
| Significance inflation ("stands as a testament to") | The number, the name, the date. Importance shown, never asserted. |
| Fake experience ("Picture this…") | The messy specific only the person who was there knows. |

## The ten judgments (questions to ask of every sentence)

1. Would you say this to one founder across a table? If it needs a stage, cut it.
2. Is the proof inside the sentence — a name, a number, a thing that happened?
3. Can the reader mime the verb?
4. Spend the rare word like money: one exact term of art per passage, in a plain sentence.
5. Let "is" be the verb.
6. One punchline per page, and it must be true.
7. Buttons and nav are furniture, not voice: verb-object or bare noun ("See the work,"
   "Email me," "About," "Send"). Every studied company converges here; nobody writes a clever
   button. Personality lives in body copy.
8. End on the last new thing. No recaps.
9. Commit or stay silent. No hedge+hype.
10. Variance with intent is the human signature — and it's produced in revision (fast bad
    draft, many rewrites, read aloud), never in draft one.

## What this means for the existing copy drafts

The `portfolio-plan/copy/` drafts are already close to this register. The revision pass
should specifically hunt: em-dash density (the current drafts use them heavily as connectors),
"not X but Y" scaffolds, triads, and any sentence that performs rather than states. The
existing hero lines ("👋 Hey, I'm Mert." / the clicking/living line) already pass every test
here — which is presumably why they were the reference.
